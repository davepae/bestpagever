I — What you are seeing

Solar wind, a hundred and fifty million kilometres from home, meets the Earth’s magnetic field and falls toward the poles. Oxygen begins to *glow* *green.* Higher, it *blushes* *red.* Nitrogen answers in *violet.* Nothing here is painted. Every pixel is *calculated,* right now, in your browser.

II — The palette

Three colours, two atoms.

Every aurora is a spectrum of exact wavelengths — the fingerprints of air struck by the Sun. Hover a colour to tune the sky to it.

557.7nm

Oxygen · 100–240 km

The emerald that defines the aurora. Excited oxygen holds its breath for about seven-tenths of a second before letting this green go.

630.0nm

Oxygen · above 240 km

In thin, high air, oxygen can linger nearly two minutes before glowing crimson — so red only survives where collisions are rare.

427.8nm

Ionised nitrogen · ~100 km

The violet-blue hem stitched along the lower edge of the brightest displays, when the solar wind hits hardest.
